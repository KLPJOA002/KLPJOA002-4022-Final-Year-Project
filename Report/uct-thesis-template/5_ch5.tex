The characterisation was performed in two stages. In the first stage the camera was tested against flat planar targets. \subsection{Capture procedure and metrics}

All planar characterisation used custom C++ software built on the Intel RealSense SDK, following the plane-fit metrics of Intel's Depth Quality Tool. Details of each trial, such as distance, tilt angle and a lux reading, are entered into the tool and the scan is started. A \gls{roi} is then applied whose pixel size is adjusted dynamically with distance so that it always covers a fixed 50\,$\times$\,50\,cm area of the target. A plane is fitted to the ROI points of every frame and compared with the ground-truth plane. Several metrics are reported by the tool, however there are four main metrics that will be discussed here to represent the general performance, each averaged over the valid frames of a trial.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Median depth error} (mm): the median signed difference between the measured and ground-truth depth, which measures systematic bias.
	
	\item \textbf{Depth RMSE} (mm): the root mean square representing depth noise from camera to plane.
	
	\item \textbf{Plane-fit RMS} (mm): the root-mean-square distance of the ROI points from the best-fit plane. This measures spatial noise and is independent of any constant offset in the depth.
	
	\item \textbf{Valid Pixels} (\%): the proportion of ROI pixels that returned a valid depth value.
	%\item \textbf{Plane-fit angle} (\degree): the tilt of the fitted plane, used to confirm the actual camera orientation.
\end{itemize}

The distinction between precision and bias matters for this project. A smooth, systematic bias is removed when the fused surface is detrended to extract microtopography, whereas random noise adds directly to the measured roughness. Plane-fit RMS and fill rate are therefore treated as the primary metric, with depth error and depth RMSE treated as secondary indicators. Each condition was recorded as a single trial. The frames within a trial capture temporal variation but not the repeatability of re-setting the experiment.

\subsection{Planar Characterisation Methodology}

\subsubsection{Working distance}
%\label{sec5.2:distance}

The camera was mounted on a tripod and pointed at a white, featureless wall, as recommended by Intel for depth-quality testing. This forces the camera to depend on its own projected infrared pattern. The tripod was used instead of the experimental rig because the rig's mounting brackets could not hold the camera sufficiently parallel to the floor. The camera angle varied with every scan and consistently exceeded 1.5\degree, which would have contaminated a distance sweep. The camera was positioned at eight distances between 604\,mm and 903\,mm, and the angle between the camera and the wall was minimised by hand at each position. The fitted-plane angle confirmed that the residual pitch and roll stayed below 0.5\degree in all trials. The ground-truth distance was measured between the wall and the cameras glass front using a tape measure.

\subsubsection{Camera tilt}
%label{sec5.3:tilt}

The camera was mounted on the experimental rig so that its angle could be set with the tilt servo. A large flat white foam board was placed on the floor and re-centred in the \gls{fov} after each change of angle. Two rotations were tested independently. The pitch axis is parallel to the line through the two IR imagers, and the roll axis is perpendicular to both the pitch axis and the optical axis. The angle was changed in steps of approximately 2\degree, covering pitch angles from $-27$\degree\ to $-2$\degree\ and roll angles from $+3$\degree\ to $+25$\degree\ for 26 trials in total. The camera mount height was kept fixed. For each trial the vertical height of the left IR imager, which is the centre of the resulting depth image, above the board was measured, and the axial ground-truth distance was calculated from that height and the tilt angle. This axial distance varied between 657\,mm and 743\,mm over the sweep. The angle of the fitted plane agreed with the set angle to within 1\degree\ in every trial (mean 0.4\degree), which verifies the angle settings.

\subsubsection{Ambient lighting}
%\label{sec:lighting}

The same foam board was scanned at a camera height of 902\,mm under a range of lighting conditions, with the camera exposure left on automatic. Indoor conditions ranged from a dark room in the evening (blinds closed, lights off) to an afternoon room with the blinds open and the lights on. Outdoor trials were performed around midday, in direct sun, shaded sun and completely shaded.
The outdoor trials were taken twice per condition, one with the foam board, and one without the foam board. This was done as the effects of the high ambient light resulted in exposure settings that were unable to detect the cameras built in projected pattern, and thus the featureless white surface lost detection. The brick flooring was able to be detected in this condition, thus the results for the outdoor trials provide relational performance compare to the rest of the trials.

\subsection{Discussion}\label{ch6:sec:discusion}

\subsubsection{Working Distance}
Stereo theory predicts that depth noise grows with the square of the range, yet the data in \autoref{fig:Scatter-Metric-Vs-Height} follow closer to linear trend. Two factors likely contribute, firstly the ambient light was not constant as the trials were taken between 15:40 and 17:35, and the auto-exposure value fell from about 5300 for the three closest distances to between 1700 and 2050 for the rest. Second, the 604 to 903\,mm range is narrow, so only a short, locally near-linear section of the quadratic curve is sampled. The practical trend is nonetheless clear. Plane-fit RMS (\autoref{fig:Scatter-Metric-Vs-Height-3}) increases with working distance while the proportion of valid pixels (\autoref{fig:Scatter-Metric-Vs-Height-4}) decreases, so the camera should be operated as close to the surface as the required field of view allows. Expressed as a percentage of distance, the median depth error rises only slightly, from 0.77\% to 0.90\%, with a weak fit (R\textsuperscript{2} = 0.18). This approximately constant relative error is consistent with a fixed bias or scale error rather than a distance-dependent effect, and is removed by detrending.

\subsubsection{Camera Tilt}

Depth noise is expected to rise with tilt angle, as the surface is foreshortened and its depth varies across each matching window. A similar relationship is visible in the plane-fit RMS and valid pixels (\autoref{fig:Scatter-Metric-Vs-Angle-3} and \autoref{fig:Scatter-Metric-Vs-Angle-4}), which both degrade as the tilt increases, with the steepest change beyond about 15\degree. Although the trend should follow a sinusoidal shape, a quadratic trend wa fitted to avoid overfitting with higher-order polynomials. Despite this mismatch, the trends describe both metrics well (R\textsuperscript{2} $\geq$ 0.95). The median depth error and depth RMSE (\autoref{fig:Scatter-Metric-Vs-Angle-1} and \autoref{fig:Scatter-Metric-Vs-Angle-2}) show only weak trends, and neighbouring angles differ by up to 6\,mm, which points to the repeatability of re-positioning the board and measuring its height as the dominant uncertainty in these metrics. Because the camera pivots at a fixed mount height, tilting it also increases the axial distance to the board, so these results combine the effects of angle and range, which does not isolate angle alone. This is accepted here, since the mount height is also fixed when scanning sediment, so the results reflect in situ conditions.

%Because the camera pivots, tilting it also increases the axial distance to the board. When the power law from the distance sweep is applied to the measured axial distance of each trial, the predicted plane-fit RMS matches the observed value to within about 7\% (for example 2.42\,mm predicted against 2.30\,mm observed at $-27.3$\degree, and 2.04\,mm against 2.12\,mm at $+25.3$\degree). The increase in noise with tilt over $\pm25$\degree\ can therefore be explained almost entirely by the longer range, and tilt itself does not appear to degrade the depth significantly. This is the result needed to justify multi-view scanning, since oblique views add information about occluded relief at little cost in noise. The scatter in depth error between neighbouring angles, which is larger than any trend, points to the repeatability of the experimental set-up (re-positioning the board and measuring its height) as the dominant source of uncertainty in this metric, and the depth-error values should be read with that in mind. A flat board cannot reveal the effects of foreshortening and self-occlusion on relief, which are examined with the 3D reference objects.

\subsubsection{Ambient Lighting}

Two conclusions follow. First, ambient light degrades active stereo depth even well within the sensor's range, because the projected infrared pattern must compete with ambient light for contrast. Daylight appears more damaging per lux than artificial light (\autoref{fig:Scatter-Metric-Vs-Light-3} and \autoref{fig:Scatter-Metric-Vs-Light-4}): 91\,lx of daylight gave 4.14\,mm RMS and 97.0\% valid pixels, against 2.84\,mm and 99.3\% at 65\,lx of fluorescent light. This is consistent with the greater near-infrared content of sunlight, so illuminance in lux is only a rough predictor of performance. Second, the surface matters as much as the illumination. The board failed completely at 2\,650\,lx in sun-exposed conditions, whereas the textured ground returned complete, low-noise data at 9\,800\,lx, so the failure cannot be attributed to light level alone. A bright, featureless white surface is a worst case for stereo matching, and the planar results should therefore be read as pessimistic bounds on the noise expected on naturally textured sediment. Wet or glossy sediment, which is more specular, was not tested outdoors and may behave worse. These results support keeping the shading tarp on the rig to limit the ambient infrared reaching the scan area.

\subsection{Discussion}

\subsubsection{Camera Configuration}
Resolution had the clearest effect of the stream settings (\autoref{fig:Scatter-Metric-Vs-Res}). For the large egg box, increasing it from the default 848\,$\times$\,480 to 1280\,$\times$\,720 reduced the RMSE by 0.32\,mm (13.4\%) and raised completeness by 9.6 percentage points. This is expected, since depth precision scales with the focal length in pixels. Unexpectedly, the pyramid outperformed the large egg box at every resolution. Sharp edges are usually smoothed more than gentle curves, but the egg box packs more features into its footprint, so more of its surface lies near curvature the camera cannot resolve.

The depth unit had only a small effect (\autoref{fig:Scatter-Metric-Vs-Depth-Units}), even on the inclined facets of the pyramid, which were expected to benefit most from finer quantisation. Accuracy is therefore limited by sensor noise rather than quantisation. The 0.1\,mm unit performed best and costs nothing within the working range, although coarser units perform almost as well.

The High Density preset performed best on both objects (\autoref{fig:Scatter-Metric-Vs-Preset}), consistent with its aim of maximising valid pixels and so reducing holes. High Accuracy gave a low RMSE but poor completeness, and may perform better once fusion fills its holes (\autoref{ch:furtherwork}).

\subsubsection{Filters}

The post-processing filters gave only a small benefit (\autoref{fig:Filter-Comparison}; settings in \autoref{tab:filter_settings}). Stronger temporal smoothing generally improved reconstruction (\autoref{fig:Temporal-Filter-Accuracy}), while spatial filtering degraded it. Both effects are smaller than the effect of height: accuracy varies less across filter settings at one height than between heights. Since each scan is static, temporal averaging was expected to help more. A likely explanation is that the remaining error on a small object is dominated by spatially correlated error, which averaging over time cannot remove. Decimation cost little accuracy and could reduce processing time after capture. It is not recommended, however, because the loss of detail is likely to be larger on large-area scans with finer relief.

\subsubsection{Tilt and Rotation}

Multi-view fusion improved every metric at every height (\autoref{fig:Combination-Comparison}). Fusing all viewpoints raised the F-score by an average of 14.0 percentage points for the large egg box and 5.8 points for the small egg box, with no change to the hardware. The error maps (\autoref{fig:singlesmallerrormap} and \autoref{fig:fusedsmallerrormap}) show that the largest errors, around $\pm$4\,mm, remain after fusion. The noise, however, is substantially smoothed and the error is more uniformly distributed \autoref{fig:fusedsmallerrorhistogram}. For this project that matters more than absolute accuracy, because random noise adds directly to the measured slope, and so a single scan will report a rougher surface than a fused surface. This is visible on the flat floor around the object, which undulates in the single scan but is flat in the fused scan (\autoref{fig:smallwave701mmsingleside} and \autoref{fig:smallwave701mmfusedside}).

\autoref{fig:Combination-Accuracy} shows that the benefit comes mainly from the range of tilts rather than the number of views. Fusing tilts of only 0 and $\pm$25\degree\ matched the full 44-view sweep, with an F-score of 0.73 against 0.73 on the large egg box and 0.50 against 0.48 on the small one. In contrast, the same number of views at 0 and $\pm$5\degree\ reached only 0.65 and 0.44. Widely spread viewpoints therefore capture most of the benefit of fusion with lower capture time.

%This chapter characterised the Intel RealSense D456 in two stages. The planar tests showed that depth noise grows rapidly with distance (from 1.2\,mm at 604\,mm to 4.6\,mm at 903\,mm), so the working distance should be as short as the analysis region allows. Tilts of up to about $\pm25$\degree\ add little noise beyond that caused by the increased range, which supports a multi-view strategy. Ambient light increases noise, and the white foam board failed in direct sun while a textured ground surface did not, so the tarp shading and the textured nature of sediment are both important. The 3D reference tests then fix the stream configuration, filter settings and view combinations used in the remainder of the project.
