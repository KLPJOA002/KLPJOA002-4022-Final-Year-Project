The aim of the overarching project is to produce high-accuracy reconstructions of intertidal sediment from which surface roughness metrics can be extracted. The accuracy of those metrics is limited by the depth data they are computed from, so the Intel RealSense D456 was characterised before the capture and processing pipeline was finalised. The characterisation answers four practical questions about how the camera and rig should be operated in situ: at what working distance, at what viewing angle, under what ambient lighting, and with which depth-stream and filter settings the camera produces the most accurate surface.

The characterisation was performed in two stages. In the first stage the camera was tested against flat planar targets. A plane has an exact, easily measured ground truth, which allows the effect of working distance, tilt angle and ambient light to be isolated from the effect of surface shape. These tests give a baseline for sensor noise and provide the operating envelope used in the second stage. In the second stage the camera was tested against 3D-printed reference objects with known geometry (see \hyperref[Apendix:Reference_Objects]{Appendix \ref*{Apendix:Reference_Objects}}.) These tests identify the camera configuration, post-processing filter settings and combination of viewing directions that give the best reconstruction, using surface relief that is representative of the sediment of interest. Each characterisation below is presented with its method, results and a short discussion.

\subsection{Capture procedure and metrics}

All planar characterisation used custom C++ software built on the Intel RealSense SDK, following the plane-fit metrics of Intel's Depth Quality Tool. Details of each trial including: distance, tilt angle and a lux reading, are entered into the tool and the scan is started, after which approximately 600 frames are recorded. A region of interest (ROI) is then applied whose pixel size is adjusted with distance so that it always covers a fixed 50\,$\times$\,50\,cm area of the target. A plane is fitted to the ROI points of every frame and compared with the ground-truth plane. Frames flagged as repeated or as outliers were discarded, leaving roughly 400 to 600 valid frames per trial\todo{confirm the same frame rejection was applied in all three sweeps}. Several metrics are reported by the tool, however there are four main metrics that will be discussed here to represent the general performance, each averaged over the valid frames of a trial:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Plane-fit RMS} (mm): the root-mean-square distance of the ROI points from the best-fit plane. This measures spatial noise and is independent of any constant offset in the depth.
	\item \textbf{Median depth error} (mm): the median signed difference between the measured and ground-truth depth, which measures systematic bias.
	\item \textbf{Fill rate} (\%): the proportion of ROI pixels that returned a valid depth value.
	%\item \textbf{Plane-fit angle} (\degree): the tilt of the fitted plane, used to confirm the actual camera orientation.
	\item \textbf{Plane Distance Error} (mm): Error between perpendicular distance from camera to the fitted plane, and the ground truth.\todo{is this a good metric?}
\end{itemize}

The distinction between precision and bias matters for this project. A smooth, systematic bias is removed when the fused surface is detrended to extract microtopography, whereas random noise adds directly to the measured roughness. Plane-fit RMS and fill rate are therefore treated as the primary metric, with depth error and Plane Distance Error treated as secondary indicators. Each condition was recorded as a single trial. The frames within a trial capture temporal variation but not the repeatability of re-setting the experiment, which is discussed in \autoref{sec5.3:chapsummary}.

\subsection{Planar Characterisation Methodology}

\subsubsection{Working distance}
%\label{sec5.2:distance}

The camera was mounted on a tripod and pointed at a white, featureless wall, as recommended by Intel for depth-quality testing. This forces the camera to depend on its own projected infrared pattern. The tripod was used instead of the experimental rig because the rig's mounting brackets could not hold the camera sufficiently parallel to the floor: the camera angle varied with every scan and consistently exceeded 1.5\degree, which would have contaminated a distance sweep. The camera was positioned at eight distances between 604\,mm and 903\,mm, and the angle between the camera and the wall was minimised by hand at each position. The fitted-plane angle confirmed that the residual pitch and roll stayed below 0.5\degree in all trials. The ground-truth distance was measured between the wall and the cameras glass front using a tape measure.

\subsubsection{Camera tilt}
%label{sec5.3:tilt}

The camera was mounted on the experimental rig so that its angle could be set with the tilt servo. A large flat white foam board was placed on the floor and re-centred in the field of view (FOV) after each change of angle. Two rotations were tested independently. The pitch axis is parallel to the line through the two IR imagers, and the roll axis is perpendicular to both the pitch axis and the optical axis. The angle was changed in steps of approximately 2\degree, covering pitch angles from $-27$\degree\ to $-2$\degree\ and roll angles from $+3$\degree\ to $+25$\degree\ for 26 trials in total\todo{confirm that the opposite signs were not recorded, or add them}. The camera mount height was kept fixed. For each trial the vertical height of the left IR imager, the centre of the resulting depth image, above the board was measured, and the axial ground-truth distance was calculated from that height and the tilt angle. This axial distance varied between 657\,mm and 743\,mm over the sweep. The angle of the fitted plane agreed with the set angle to within 1\degree\ in every trial (mean 0.4\degree), which verifies the angle settings.

\subsubsection{Ambient lighting}
%\label{sec:lighting}

The same foam board was scanned at a camera height of approximately 900\,,m under a range of lighting conditions, with the camera exposure left on automatic. \subsection{Discusion}

\subsubsection{Working Distance}
Stereo triangulation noise is expected to grow with the square of the range. A power-law fit to these data gives an exponent of approximately 3.3, which is steeper than expected. Part of the difference may come from the ambient light, which was not constant across the sweep. The auto-exposure value dropped from around 5300 for the three closest distances to between 1700 and 2050 for the remainder, and the largest step in noise (from 1.56\,mm to 2.64\,mm) coincides with this change. The trials were recorded in a different order to their distance (between about 15:40 and 17:35), so distance and ambient light are partly confounded. Even allowing for this, the trend is clear: noise grows rapidly with working distance, so the camera should be operated at the shortest distance that still frames the analysis region. The roughly constant relative depth error of about 0.8\% is consistent with a scale or ground-truth referencing offset rather than a distance-dependent effect. It does not affect the roughness metrics, but it would appear as a scale error in an absolute reconstruction, which the 3D reference tests in Section~\ref{sec:reference} quantify. 

\subsubsection{Camera Tilt}

Because the camera pivots, tilting it also increases the axial distance to the board. When the power law from the distance sweep is applied to the measured axial distance of each trial, the predicted plane-fit RMS matches the observed value to within about 7\% (for example 2.42\,mm predicted against 2.30\,mm observed at $-27.3$\degree, and 2.04\,mm against 2.12\,mm at $+25.3$\degree). The increase in noise with tilt over $\pm25$\degree\ can therefore be explained almost entirely by the longer range, and tilt itself does not appear to degrade the depth significantly. This is the result needed to justify multi-view scanning, since oblique views add information about occluded relief at little cost in noise. The scatter in depth error between neighbouring angles, which is larger than any trend, points to the repeatability of the experimental set-up (re-positioning the board and measuring its height) as the dominant source of uncertainty in this metric, and the depth-error values should be read with that in mind. A flat board cannot reveal the effects of foreshortening and self-occlusion on relief, which are examined with the 3D reference objects.

\subsubsection{Ambient Lighting}

Two conclusions follow. First, ambient light degrades the depth from active stereo even when the surface is well within the sensor's range, because the projected infrared pattern has to compete with the ambient light for contrast. Daylight appears more damaging per lux than artificial light (4.14\,mm at 91\,lux of daylight against 2.84\,mm at 65\,lux of artificial light), which is consistent with the greater near-infrared content of sunlight, so illuminance in lux is only a rough predictor of performance. Second, the surface matters as much as the illumination. The board failed at 2\,650\,lux, whereas the ground surface returned complete, low-noise data at 9\,800\,lux, so the failure cannot be attributed to the light level alone. A bright, featureless, highly reflective white surface is a worst case for stereo matching. The ground surface supplies natural texture, so the planar results on white targets should be read as pessimistic bounds on the noise expected on sediment. The outdoor tests did not include a matched pair of lux levels for the two surfaces, and wet or glossy sediment, which is more specular, was not tested. These results support retaining the shading tarp on the rig, which the Chapter~\ref{ch:system} design already includes, to limit the ambient infrared reaching the scan area\todo{check the label of the System Design chapter}.

\section{3D Reference Characterisation}
\label{sec5.2:reference}
%=====================================================================
The planar tests establish the sensor's noise behaviour but not how accurately it reconstructs three-dimensional relief, which is affected by resolution, filtering, foreshortening and the fusion of several views. These effects were measured against reference objects with known geometry using a modified version of the main capture program, which records scans under controlled software settings and hardware set-ups.



\subsection{Reference objects and evaluation}

Three 3D-printed reference objects were used (Appendix~\todo{ref}). Each has a 100\,$\times$\,100\,mm footprint. Two are sinusoidal ``egg carton'' surfaces of the form $z = \sin(x) + \sin(y)$ at a large scale (feature spacing 50\,mm) and a small scale (25\,mm), and the third is a square-based pyramid of 50\,mm height\todo{confirm the egg carton amplitude and wavelength against the CAD drawings}. The smooth sinusoidal surfaces approximate the mounds produced by sand prawns, whose heights of 3 to 5\,cm were reported in the literature review, and the small carton tests the lateral resolution of the camera. The pyramid has sharp edges and planar facets, which show whether the filters preserve geometric edges or smooth them.

Scans were evaluated with the \texttt{evaluate\_scans} tool described in Chapter~\ref{ch:system}\todo{Section ref}. The tool rigidly aligns each scan to the reference .stl model, using feature-matching starts followed by point-to-plane ICP, and then computes point-to-surface distances. Scale is not estimated, so any scale error in the camera data appears as geometric error. The metrics used are RMSE, mean absolute error, bias and standard deviation of the point-to-surface distance, the completeness, precision and F-score at distance thresholds of 0.5, 1, 2 and 5\,mm, and the fraction of the reference surface covered. Every evaluation logs its parameters, reference hash and random seed, so all results can be reproduced and compared directly.

\subsection{Testing Procedure}

\subsubsection{Camera configuration}

The first sweep fixed the height and tilt of the camera and scanned the large egg carton and the pyramid with different stream configurations. The resolution ranged from 1280\,$\times$\,720 to 640\,$\times$\,360 pixels, the depth units from 0.001\,m to 0.0001\,m, and all of the built-in visual presets were tested except the Hand preset, which is intended for gesture tracking. The depth unit sets the quantisation step of the 16-bit depth values, and the finest setting (0.1\,mm) still provides a maximum range of 6.5\,m, well beyond the working distance. \todo{Results table: RMSE, completeness and F-score for each resolution, depth unit and preset on both objects, plus one or two sentences of discussion}

Based on this sweep, a resolution of 1280\,$\times$\,720, a depth unit of 0.0001\,m and the High Density preset were used for all later scans\todo{confirm that this was the outcome of the sweep}. These are the settings listed in Chapter~\ref{ch:system}.

\subsubsection{Post-processing filters}

With the configuration fixed, five stationary scans of each object were recorded at each of the tested heights, without tilt or rotation. The data were recorded to file so that the RealSense post-processing filters could be applied offline in many combinations to the same scans (Grunnet-Jepsen and Tong~\todo{cite}). The filters were decimation, spatial, temporal and hole-filling, and a sweep was made over their smoothing alpha, smoothing delta and magnitude parameters\todo{list the values or ranges tested for each filter}. Decimation reduces the resolution, the spatial filter performs edge-preserving smoothing, the temporal filter averages depth across frames, and hole filling interpolates invalid pixels. Each combination was evaluated against the reference, and the process was repeated at each height to determine whether the best filter settings depend on the working distance. \todo{Results: best-performing settings per height and object, and the trade-off between noise reduction and loss of fine relief or edges}

\subsubsection{Tilt and rotation}

Finally, a full scan of each object was recorded at rotations of $-45$\degree, 0\degree, $45$\degree\ and $90$\degree\ and at tilts from $-25$\degree\ to $+25$\degree\ in steps of 5\degree. This gives 44 views of each object. Offline, these views were aligned and fused by the pipeline in Chapter~\ref{ch:pipeline}. For each view, several coarse alignment hypotheses are generated (from the IMU orientation, the detected AprilTag positions, the known rotation and tilt settings, and RANSAC matching of FPFH features) and scored. The best hypothesis is refined with ICP and the aligned scans are merged\todo{expand once the fusion method is finalised}. Subsets of the views were fused and each fusion was evaluated against the reference, which shows how the reconstruction accuracy depends on the number of views and on the combination of tilt and rotation. \todo{Results: accuracy and coverage versus the views included, and the recommended tilt and rotation set}

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec5.3:chapsummary}
%=====================================================================
This chapter characterised the Intel RealSense D456 in two stages. The planar tests showed that depth noise grows rapidly with distance (from 1.2\,mm at 604\,mm to 4.6\,mm at 903\,mm), so the working distance should be as short as the analysis region allows. Tilts of up to about $\pm25$\degree\ add little noise beyond that caused by the increased range, which supports a multi-view strategy. Ambient light increases noise, and the white foam board failed in direct sun while a textured ground surface did not, so the tarp shading and the textured nature of sediment are both important. The 3D reference tests then fix the stream configuration, filter settings and view combinations used in the remainder of the project.

The results have limitations that affect how they should be used. Each condition was recorded once, so the effect of re-setting the experiment is not quantified, and the scatter in depth error between neighbouring tilt angles suggests that it is significant. The distance sweep was not recorded in order of distance and the lighting changed between sessions, so distance and ambient light are partly confounded. The lux values come from an uncalibrated smartphone sensor. The distance sweep was performed with a tripod because the rig brackets could not hold the camera level, and the white featureless planar targets are likely to overstate the noise expected on textured sediment. Wet and specular sediment has not yet been tested. These points are taken up in Chapter~\ref{ch:further}\todo{ref to Further work}.
