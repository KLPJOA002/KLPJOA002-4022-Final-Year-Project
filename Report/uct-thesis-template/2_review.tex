\chapter{Literature review}
\label{ch:review}
 \section{Biology}
 Pillay and Branch \cite{inbook} describe how sand prawn species are prominent members in the benthic ecosystem. The sand prawns continuously change the sediment properties such as surface topography porosity and biochemistry, through actions such as burrowing and water pumping. These actions redistribute buried sediment to the surface which results in surface mounds which increase the effective surface area of the sediment by up to 15 times. These mounds can increase the effective hydrodynamic resistance which can help to stagnate the water flow close to the sediment. While the paper mentions measurements specific to sand prawn species located in other regions, they provide an insight into approximate feature sizes that can be expected, for example \textit{Callianassa subterranea} present in the North sea can produce mounds 5.4cm tall and 11.7cm wide, and \textit{Pestarella candida}, located in the Mediterranean and Adriatic Seas, which can produce mounds 3cm tall and 15cm wide.
 
 Venter et al \cite{venterWaterFiltrationBurrowing2020} further explains how sand prawns, \textit{Callianassa kraussi}, present in the South African coastlines and at the Langebaan Lagoon, function as prominent water filterers. Within the burrows they actively irrigate the sediment through bi-directional pumping of water to replenish oxygen levels within the burrows, and to remove waste products. This irrigation has a secondary effect where phytoplankton are filtered out of the water by the burrow walls. This filtering can help improve ecosystem resilience to harmful conditions such as nutrient pollution which promote phytoplankton blooms. In relation to this project, the papers methodology entails manual visual counting of burrow openings.
 
 The next question with regards to biological activity, is weather surface roughness, specifically rugosity, is a valid measurement to gauge the underlying biological activity within the benthic ecosystem. To this, Schenone et al \cite{schenoneSedimentMicrotopographyPredicts2024,schenoneSeafloorSedimentMicrotopography2025} determined experimentally that analysing two dimensional surface rugosity, they were able to correlate surface roughness measurements with sub-surface activity. The authors created a controlled laboratory environment to simulate the benthic ecosystem, which can be seen in \autoref{fig:schenonecustomincubationtank}. The pipeline involved taking images perpendicular to the sediment cross section, manually extracting the surface line distance using the image processing application ImageJ, and computing the rugosity as a ration between this measured distance, and the distance of a straight line from end to end. Whilst the authors did find a correlation, rugosity explained 35\% to 61\% of variability observed, Their implementation is still under controlled laboratory conditions. The cross sectional profile imaging is not possible in situ, and the two-dimensional rugosity calculation relies on a thin 'slice' of sediment. These limitations point to a need for three dimensional surface scans.

%   natbib gives you two forms, and they are NOT interchangeable.
%
%     \citet{key}   TEXTUAL. The author becomes part of the sentence.
%                   "As \citet{shannon1948} showed, ..."
%                   -> As Shannon (1948) showed, ...
%
%     \citep{key}   PARENTHETICAL. The whole citation sits in brackets at
%                   the end of a clause.
%                   "...is a solved problem \citep{shannon1948}."
%                   -> ...is a solved problem (Shannon, 1948).
%
%   \cite{key} behaves like \citet in author-year mode and like a numeric
%   citation in IEEE mode, so it reads differently depending on the switch at
%   the top of 0_main.tex. Prefer \citet and \citep: they always mean what
%   they say.
%
%   In IEEE mode both come out as [1], which is why IEEE citations are easier
%   to write and harder to read.
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
