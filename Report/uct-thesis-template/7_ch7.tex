\chapter{Experimental Validation}
\label{ch:Validation}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
The complete pipeline, from capture through cropping and fusion to roughness estimation, was validated in two ways. First, it was applied to scans of the 3D-printed reference objects recorded during characterisation. These objects do not resemble sediment in situ, but their geometry is known exactly, so the measured rugosity can be compared against a true value.

Second, sand taken from the Langebaan Lagoon was placed in a plastic tub and scanned in dry, wet and waterlogged states, both flattened and shaped. These surfaces have no exact ground truth, so the results are mainly qualitative, but they show how the pipeline behaves on the material and moisture conditions it is intended for.

\section{Controlled Targets}

\subsection{Process}
The roughness pipeline was applied to the two smooth reference objects, the large and small egg boxes (\autoref{Apendix:Reference_Objects}), using scans recorded at a height of 701~mm during characterisation. The best-performing filters identified in \autoref{ch:experiments} were applied, and each session was cropped and fused. The fused surface was then cropped to the footprint of the reference object, and rugosity was computed for both the fused surface and a single untilted scan, using each of the four detrending degrees (planar, quadratic, cubic and quartic). The reference surface was sampled from its .stl model and passed through the same levelling, detrending and gridding, so that the measured and reference values are directly comparable.

\subsection{Results}
The rugosity of each reconstruction and of its reference is given in \autoref{tab:Big_Egg_Rugosity} and \autoref{tab:Small_Egg_Rugosity}, and plotted in \autoref{fig:Big-Egg-Rugosity} and \autoref{fig:Small-Egg-Rugosity}. Two results stand out: the effect of the detrending degree, and the apparent advantage of single scans over fused scans.

Because the reference is detrended in the same way, its rugosity shows what each degree removes. For the large egg box it falls from 1.34 with planar and quadratic detrending to 1.27 with cubic and quartic detrending (\autoref{fig:Big-Egg-Rugosity}). Its relief spans about one wavelength across the patch, which a cubic or quartic surface can follow, so these fits remove part of the egg box shape itself (\autoref{Appendix:Validation_Results}). The relief of the small egg box spans about two wavelengths, which none of the polynomials followed, so its reference rugosity is 1.69 at every degree (\autoref{fig:Small-Egg-Rugosity}). Planar detrending is therefore used below.

With planar detrending, the single scan of the large egg box measured 1.31 and the fused scan 1.25, against a reference of 1.34. On the small egg box they measured 1.31 and 1.26, against 1.69. This appears to contradict \autoref{ch:experiments}, where fusion improved every accuracy metric, but the single scan is not more accurate. Instead, two separate errors partly cancel in it.

The first error is shared by every reconstruction. The reconstructions carry some scale error and under-represent the amplitude of the reference waveforms, so they report a lower rugosity than the true surface. Since rugosity scales approximately with the squared slope, a 10\% loss of amplitude removes about 19\% of the excess area. The fused reconstruction recovers about three quarters of the reference excess area on the large egg box, but under 40\% on the small one, whose features approach the resolution of the camera and the 10~mm MLS radius.

The second error affects single scans only. Their higher depth noise adds spurious slope to every grid cell, which artificially increases the measured roughness. This is visible on the flat floor around the small egg box, which undulates in the single scan (\autoref{fig:smallwave701mmsingleside}) but is flat in the fused scan (\autoref{fig:smallwave701mmfusedside}). The single scan lands closer to the reference only because its noise offsets part of the lost amplitude, and the fused value remains the more faithful measurement of the surface.

\section{Controlled Laboratory Sand}
\comment{Bringing the Beach to the Lab}
The reference objects allow an analytical comparison, but they are not sediment. Field deployment was not possible within the time available, so about 13~kg of dry sand from the Langebaan Lagoon was placed in a 10~L plastic tub, flattened by hand so that its rugosity should be close to 1, and scanned. It was then indented with the reference objects and scanned again. Next, 2~kg of water (about 15\% of the dry mass) was added to approximate wet intertidal sediment, and the flattened and indented scans were repeated. Finally, more water was added until it pooled on the surface, and the sand was shaped by hand into an approximately natural texture and scanned once more. Each session was processed both as a fused reconstruction and as a single scan.

\subsection{Results}
The rugosity of each surface is given in \autoref{tab:Sand_Rugosity} and \autoref{fig:Sand-Rugosity}, and the flat and soaked reconstructions are shown in \autoref{fig:Sand-Scans}. These surfaces have no exact ground truth, but they behave as expected. Flattened surfaces measured close to 1, indented surfaces well above it, and the hand-shaped soaked surface between the two. The spread across the four detrending degrees was at most 0.001.

The flattened surfaces give the clearest result. With almost no relief whose amplitude could be lost, the noise effect dominates and the expected ordering appears. Dry flat sand measured 1.0034 fused, against 1.0193 as a single scan. Since a flattened surface cannot have a rugosity below 1, these values bound the contribution of noise, at under 0.34\% excess area for a fused scan against up to 1.9\% for a single scan. The single-scan noise is visible as a fine texture across the surface (\autoref{fig:Sand-DryFlat-Single}) that fusion largely removes (\autoref{fig:Sand-DryFlat-Fused}). Wet flat sand followed the same pattern, at 1.0093 fused and 1.0243 single.

On the indented surfaces, single scans added 2.1 (dry) and 2.6 (wet) percentage points of excess area over the fused scans, slightly more than on flat sand, which is consistent with noise rising with obliquity (\autoref{ch:experiments}). Against the 5.8 to 5.9\% excess area of the fused scans, this is an error of a third to nearly half of the relief signal itself.

On the wet flat surface, a large region of high noise appears in the centre of the scan (\autoref{fig:Sand-WetFlat-Single},\autoref{fig:Sand-WetFlat-Fused}). It is attributed to specular reflection of the infrared projector from the water film, which returns the pattern directly into the imagers where the bed faces the camera, directly beneath it. The rig tilts and rotates the camera about an almost fixed position, so this point is the same in every view and fusion is not expected to remove it. This is consistent with the fused wet flat surface measuring almost three times the excess area of the dry flat surface (0.93\% against 0.34\%). Multi-view capture alone therefore does not prevent this artefact, and such reflections must be avoided on wet, flat sediment.

The soaked surface is the closest to waterlogged sediment in situ. Despite the pooled water, the fused reconstruction still follows the hand-shaped texture (\autoref{fig:Sand-Soaked-Fused}), so the system remains usable on saturated sediment. Here, however, the single and fused scans differed by only 0.26 percentage points, either because the single scan was less noisy on this surface or because its errors over the pooled water were common to every view and so survived fusion.
