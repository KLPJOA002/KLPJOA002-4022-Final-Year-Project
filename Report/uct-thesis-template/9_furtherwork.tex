\chapter{Further work}
\label{ch:furtherwork}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
The 12-week timeframe limited how thoroughly parts of the system could be characterised and validated. This chapter identifies where the results are weakest and the work that would address them.

Illuminance was read with an uncalibrated smartphone sensor, and lux does not describe spectral content. Daylight degraded the depth data more per lux than fluorescent light, which is consistent with its larger near-infrared component, so the indoor results do not fully represent conditions in situ. The outdoor trials were also not directly comparable with the indoor ones, since the featureless foam board failed in direct sunlight and the brick floor had to be scanned instead. A repeat characterisation should use a calibrated light meter, ideally with a near-infrared or spectral measurement, and scan both a textureless board and a texture-rich surface under every condition, so that the effects of illumination and texture can be separated.

Ambient light also drifted during the long sweeps. Over the working-distance sweep (15:40 to 17:35) the auto-exposure value fell from about 5300 to between 1700 and 2050, so lighting is confounded with distance. The height and tilt sweeps should be repeated under controlled lighting, or in a randomised order.

Each condition was recorded once, so the repeatability of the set-up is unknown. The spread reported for the laboratory sand reflects processing options rather than repeat captures. Repeated captures of key conditions are needed, as is further testing on wet sand, where specular reflection from a water film produced a region of high noise. The system has also not been deployed on the Langebaan intertidal flats. Field trials within a low-tide window are needed to confirm the recommended protocol, and would provide the data on which to develop automated detection of mounds and burrow openings, which was removed from the scope of this project.

On the reference objects, single scans gave rugosity closer to the reference than fused scans, in conflict with every other metric. This is attributed to the reconstructions under-representing relief amplitude while single-scan noise adds slope, but the explanation has not been tested directly. A flat reference plate, processed with the same grid spacing and smoothing, would establish the noise floor and allow the pipeline's noise correction to be applied. Reference objects with lower-amplitude and shorter-wavelength relief would show where amplitude is lost, including the effect of the 10\,mm MLS radius. Larger surfaces with verified roughness would give a more representative validation. The High Accuracy preset, which gave low RMSE but poor completeness in single scans, should also be tested in combination with fusion, which can fill its holes.

The D456 has a 95\,mm baseline optimised for longer range \cite{intel_d456}. Its minimum depth of about 0.52\,m at 1280\,$\times$\,720, together with the depth anomalies observed at 687\,mm with tilts of around 17\textdegree, prevents it from operating at the shorter distances where noise is lowest. Shorter-baseline cameras are better suited to close range. The D415 has a minimum range of about 160\,mm \cite{carfagniMetrologicalCriticalCharacterization2019} and gave the best short-range reconstructions of the cameras compared by \citet{serviMetrologicalCharacterizationComparison2021}, while the D435 kept errors below 1\,cm within 1\,m \cite{rustlerEmpiricalComparisonFour2025}. These should be compared with the D456 on the same reference objects. Finally, an external projector can add texture beyond the camera's internal pattern \cite{ProjectorsD400Series}, which may improve reconstruction of featureless or wet surfaces, but this could not be investigated within the timeframe.
